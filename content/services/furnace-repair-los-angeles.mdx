Los Angeles winters are mild — but the few weeks each year when overnight lows drop into the 30s and 40s are when furnaces fail. The system sat idle for 8–9 months. Dust settled on the flame sensor. Mice nested in the burner compartment. A capacitor that was marginal last spring finally gave up on the first cold night.

## What Goes Wrong With Furnaces in LA Homes

The flame sensor is the single most common failure point. After months of inactivity, surface oxidation prevents the sensor from detecting flame, and the system shuts down within seconds of starting. Cleaning or replacing the sensor — a $200–$400 service visit — resolves this on the spot.

Hot surface igniters are second. They operate at 1,800°F+ and develop hairline cracks over time. A cracked igniter glows but doesn't produce enough heat to ignite gas reliably.

Inducer motor failures, control board failures, and gas valve issues are less common but more expensive. These typically run $500–$900 to repair.

Heat exchanger cracks are the failure mode you cannot ignore. A cracked heat exchanger releases combustion byproducts — including carbon monoxide — into the air supply. A C-20 licensed technician will not run a furnace with a cracked heat exchanger; it is repaired, replaced, or the furnace is shut down until resolved.

## When to Replace Rather Than Repair

Furnaces typically last 18–25 years. In LA's mild climate, with light annual usage, well-maintained furnaces sometimes run 25–30 years. The decision points to consider replacement: heat exchanger failure on a unit over 15 years old, control board failure on a unit over 20, or repeated repairs in consecutive seasons that combined exceed 40–50% of replacement cost.

A modern high-efficiency furnace (95% AFUE+) runs efficiently enough that — in a home that uses heating regularly — the utility savings can offset the price difference versus a standard 80% AFUE replacement within 6–8 years. In LA, where total heating hours are limited, the calculation is closer; standard-efficiency replacements often make better financial sense.

## Safety First

If you smell gas, leave the home and call your utility company before calling for furnace service. If your CO detector activates, leave the home, call 911, and do not re-enter until the home is ventilated and the furnace is inspected. These are emergencies — not service calls.

For everything else — system not turning on, weak heat output, unusual noises, frequent cycling — schedule a diagnostic visit. Most furnace issues in LA homes are routine repairs, not safety incidents.
